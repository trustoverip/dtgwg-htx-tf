Human Trust Experience — Research findings

A synthesis of trust-experience design: a thirteen-moment journey model (five phases, crossed with six UX domains) with a per-moment calibration posture, twenty best practices and exemplar interfaces from mainstream products, an anti-pattern register, prescriptive rules from named authorities, and the mapping onto the four-layer Iceberg model.

The ten principles

01Design for calibration, not for trust.
The goal is not maximal trust but accurate trust: a user whose confidence matches the system's real reliability. Over-trust (automation misuse) and under-trust (disuse) are both failures. Every pattern in this library is ultimately a calibration device — it moves the user's mental model toward the ground truth of what the system can and cannot do.
  • Lee & See (2004), Trust in Automation — 'calibrated trust'.
  • Parasuraman & Riley (1997) — misuse / disuse / abuse.
  • Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995) — Ability / Benevolence / Integrity.
02Authenticate the verifier to the human before you ask the human for anything.
Wallet identity inverts the classic login: the relying party must prove who it is to the user first, and the interface is the only place that inversion becomes real. Render a compact identity card — legal name, jurisdiction, the authority that vouches for it, the purpose it is registered for — checked against the trust list before the consent screen is ever drawn.
  • EUDI ARF v2.x — access vs registration certificate.
  • Trust moment T4 (Counterparty identification), the market's weakest.
  • Korir, Parkin & Dunphy (2022), SOUPS.
03Three trust states minimum, and the middle one must be honest.
Binary indicators train users to read the absence of a warning as the presence of safety — the implied truth effect. At least three states are needed, and the middle one must admit uncertainty in plain language: 'the identity of the signer can't be verified.' C2PA's middle-state wording is the exemplar to copy.
  • C2PA UX Guidance for Implementers v2.2 (CC BY 4.0).
  • Implied truth effect — an open user-research question flagged in the C2PA spec.
04Show the proposition, not the data field.
Render the claim being proven and its truth value, not a field with a value: 'Over 18: Yes. Your date of birth is not shared.' The negative clause teaches the privacy property while exercising it. Presenting the credential as an indivisible object throws away the cryptographic advantage and reproduces the all-or-nothing plastic card.
  • eIDAS 2.0 (Reg. (EU) 2024/1183) Art. 5a — selective disclosure.
  • Use case UC-01: age assurance without DoB — the best-solved interaction in existence.
05Never render a hash where a sentence belongs.
Blind signing is the canonical trust-UX failure: never ask a person to sign or approve what they cannot read. Render the effect of an action in human language; keep 'show raw' as an expert affordance, never the default. The EIP-712 / ERC-7730 / ERC-8213 stack exists precisely to make this possible.
  • EIP-712 (typed data); ERC-7730 (display metadata); ERC-8213 (wallet display).
  • Anti-pattern AP-04: hash as description.
06Simulate the outcome. Show what will be true afterward.
Shift the screen from 'do you authorize this instruction?' to 'here is what will be true afterward.' Simulating balance and permission deltas before commit turns a parsing task into a comparison task, which humans do far better. This crypto-wallet reframing is the single largest unexploited pattern transfer into identity disclosure.
  • Open question: does transaction simulation transfer to identity disclosure? (untested).
  • Trust moment T5 (Request comprehension).
07Label computed facts differently from asserted claims.
Distinguish assertions the system produced under its own control (trusted timestamps, device-signed GPS) from assertions gathered from elsewhere (imported EXIF, typed captions). Any interface that mixes measured, derived and declared inputs into one visual treatment is lying by typography. This transfers directly to AI trust scoring.
  • C2PA: 'recorded by the capture device' vs 'entered by the creator'.
  • Directly applicable to Validant's AI trust-score surface.
08Spend friction on irreversibility, and nowhere else.
Confirmation friction is a finite budget, not a free safety measure. Assign each action an irreversibility cost and spend friction against it: reversible low-stakes actions get none; irreversible ones get typed confirmation, re-auth, or a delay. Warnings are a scarce resource — a target warning rate should be treated like a crash rate.
  • Alert-fatigue / habituation literature.
  • Anti-pattern AP-05: warning inflation.
09Every disclosure leaves a receipt the person owns.
Every disclosure event should produce a durable, human-readable, exportable record: timestamp, counterparty, exact attributes released, legal basis, retention. It is the only thing that makes a subject-access request tractable, the only evidence in a dispute, and the only way a person can learn their own disclosure behaviour. Granting and withdrawing must cost the same effort.
  • Use case UC-10: disclosure history — the biggest gap vs regulatory promise.
  • DSA Art. 25; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022; CPPA v. American Honda (2025).
10Say what the system does not prove.
Trust systems accumulate over-attribution in the interface, so the interface is where it must be corrected — in one plain sentence, at the summary level, not buried in an FAQ. Provenance is not veracity; a verified issuer is not a good actor; a trust score is not a guarantee. C2PA does not detect deepfakes or establish truth, and the UI must say so.
  • C2PA scope disclaimers (provenance ≠ veracity).
  • Anti-pattern AP-11: sovereignty without affordances.
  • Finding: the implied truth effect.

The trust iceberg

Trust UX has four layers. The visible tip (Agency, Engineering) is usually designed well; the Governance layer straddling the waterline is where most products fail, and the Institutional base is rarely designed at all.

Iceberg elements, defined
  • Reciprocity. Fair, transparent value exchange: rewarding good actions and easing user concern through fairness. The single strongest cue effect on trusting beliefs.
  • Brand. Intangible identity, reputation and consistency. Investing in a brand signals trustworthiness as capital put at risk: a costly, hard-to-fake signal.
  • Technical Trust Infrastructure. The identity, privacy, security and compliance machinery. The interface between the visible cues and the hidden foundations of trust.
  • Social Trust Mechanisms. Community-driven trust: reputation systems, endorsements, moderation and social proof.
  • Governance, Resilience & Assurance. How the organisation keeps trust up over time: adaptive governance, operational resilience, and continuous assurance through audits, monitoring and certifications.
  • Contextual Moderation Layer. The waterline itself. Perceived risk and context (magnitude, culture, domain, user segment) decide which cues are visible and how high a belief must clear before it becomes action.
  • Institution-based trust. Trust in the surrounding system or structure even without prior interaction: structural assurance and situational normality.
  • Trusting Beliefs. The trustor's assessment of the other party, held on two lenses that context switches between: reasoned cognition and felt affect.
  • Cognitive beliefs. Reasoned judgement: competence, benevolence, integrity and predictability for a person; functionality, reliability and helpfulness for a system.
  • Affective beliefs. Emotional trust grounded in empathy, attachment and the felt quality of the relationship.
  • Disposition to Trust. The individual's baseline propensity to trust, shaped by personality and past experience: the same cue lands differently on different people.
  • Trusting Intentions & Behaviors. The willingness to act on trust (buy, share data, delegate) and the act itself: the behavioural outcome every other layer feeds into.
  • Trust Formation. How trust is first built: calculus-based (cost versus benefit), then knowledge-based (from repeated interaction), then identification-based (shared values).
  • Trust Calibration. Keeping perceived trust aligned with actual trustworthiness over time: are the cues relevant, available, detected and used correctly? Where automation bias and algorithm aversion live.
  • Trust Repair. How trust recovers after a violation: competence failures need an apology and a fix; integrity failures need denial plus evidence of principle. The wrong move makes it terminal.

The calibration model

A trust moment is a decision under uncertainty; the goal is calibrated reliance, not maximal trust. Each moment below carries a default posture. Every increase in authority, disclosure or irreversibility must be matched by an increase in evidence, clarity or control.

Increase Meaningful new evidence supports competence, integrity, provenance or control.
Hold The interaction is routine; no new evidence justifies broader confidence.
Decrease A mismatch, unexplained change, excessive request or poor outcome appears.
Suspend Uncertainty is unresolved and the possible consequence exceeds an acceptable threshold.

The journey, end to end

The same thirteen moments, now in sequence and grouped into five phases. Phase C recurs on every transaction; Phase D runs continuously; Phase E is reactive.

A Onboarding & FormationGetting in and forming the first relationships
  • 1. Invitation & OnboardingHold

    Maya taps an invite from a friend already in the network. The screen names the inviter, explains what the First Person Network is, and makes clear that accepting is not a bare signup but the start of her first trust relationship. DID mechanics stay hidden.

  • 2. Custody & Recovery SetupIncrease

    Before holding anything valuable, her wallet is created; she learns in plain language where her key lives (never shown as a raw seed phrase) and nominates two guardians, so recovery exists before she ever needs it.

  • 3. Relationship Formation & RecognitionHold

    Accepting runs the pairwise-DID handshake with the inviter over a private channel; both receive a human-readable r-card and exchange the bidirectional VRC pair, exactly like adding a contact on Signal.

B Credentials & IssuanceReceiving credentials, and issuing to peers
  • 4. Enrollment / IssuanceIncrease

    Maya provides evidence and receives her Personhood Credential from a verified issuer (evidence, checks, PHC issued); holder-binding is made perceptible so she trusts it is genuinely hers.

  • 5. Peer Issuance & VouchingHold

    Acting now as an issuer, Maya vouches for a classmate by issuing a skill attestation; the UI shows precisely what she is attesting and that she, not just the holder, is on record for it.

C The Live CeremonyThe recurring proof, once per transaction
  • 6. Counterparty Identification & VerificationrecurringHold

    A rental platform asks her to prove she is a real, unique person. Before revealing anything she confirms WHO is asking (verified verifier vs proceed-with-caution) and sees its reputation; the browser-to-wallet redirect is announced and frictionless.

  • 7. Request ComprehensionrecurringHold

    She reads exactly what is requested and why, in plain language, with nothing hidden behind a blind signature.

  • 8. Persona SelectionrecurringHold

    She chooses which persona to present, her public/rental persona rather than her private one, and confirms it will not correlate with her other selves.

  • 9. Consent & Disclosure DecisionrecurringHold

    The hero screen: via ZKP she discloses only 'unique person + over 18' without revealing her name or birth date. The decline / share-less path is exactly as prominent and as short as proceeding.

  • 10. Execution & ConfirmationrecurringIncrease

    She confirms; the proof is sent, the platform shows clear success, and she keeps a receipt of exactly what she shared and with whom, working the same online or offline.

D Delegation & Standing AccessRuns continuously thereafter
  • 11. Agent Delegation & Standing AccessstandingHold

    She authorizes a personal AI agent to renew such proofs on her behalf; she sets scope, sees the agent works for HER, keeps human-in-the-loop on sensitive steps, and the system re-confirms this standing access periodically and pauses if she does not respond.

E Overview & LifecycleStanding overview and reactive repair
  • 12. Retrospection & PortfoliostandingDecrease

    A dashboard shows her relationships as r-cards, credentials, personas and their correlation exposure, and a log of everything shared, in human terms; she calmly revokes one routine grant she no longer needs.

  • 13. Incident, Recovery & RevocationreactiveSuspend

    She loses her phone. She hits no dead-end: she recovers her keys via her guardians (recovery) and, separately, revokes a VRC she had issued to someone she no longer trusts (revocation), two clearly labeled and distinct flows.

Twenty best practices

Layer A · Making the counterparty legible

BP-01 Verifier identity card before the ask
10/10

Before showing any data request, render a compact identity card for the requesting party: legal name, jurisdiction, the authority that vouches for it, and the credential it operates under, checked against the trust list before the consent screen is drawn.

Why. It inverts the phishing economics: in wallet identity the relying party must authenticate to the user first, and the UI is the only place that inversion becomes real.

Use case. Scan a QR at a car-rental desk: 'Europcar International SA, registered in France, access certificate issued by ANTS, registered to request: driving-licence category, name, age over 21.'

T4 Counterparty Identification & Verification
BP-02 Three-state trust display, never binary
10/10

Trust status needs at least three states, and the middle one must be honest: Verified, Unknown/unverifiable, Flagged/invalid. C2PA's middle-state language is exemplary: 'the identity of the signer can't be verified.'

Why. Binary indicators train users to read absence of warning as presence of safety (the implied truth effect).

Use case. A Content Credentials L1 badge expands to 'Signed by [org], but we could not confirm this signer against a recognized authority.'

T4 Counterparty Identification & Verification
BP-03 Purpose binding, mismatch as a first-class warning
9/10

The EUDI architecture separates the access certificate (who you are) from the registration certificate (what you're registered to ask for). When the declared scope and the actual request diverge, that divergence is the highest-value fact on screen.

Why. It converts a governance-registry artifact into an actionable consumer protection at the exact moment of vulnerability.

Use case. 'Registered to request: age over 18. Currently asking for: full name, date of birth, home address. Three attributes fall outside its registered purpose.' With a one-tap report path.

T4 Counterparty Identification & VerificationT5 Request Comprehension
BP-04 Name the trust source, not just the verdict
8/10

Every trust assertion should be traceable in one tap to who says so. 'Verified issuer' expands to 'Listed on the Swiss federal Base Registry since 2025-03-14.'

Why. The difference between a trust mark and a trust claim with provenance; it also degrades gracefully as timestamps age.

Use case. Tap 'Trusted' to reveal the trust list, its publisher, and last-update timestamp.

T4 Counterparty Identification & Verification

Layer B · Making the ask legible

BP-05 Attribute-level disclosure, minimum-necessary defaults
10/10

The request screen lists individual claims, not a credential. Each claim is a row; optional claims are toggles default-off; required claims are marked with the reason inline.

Why. Presenting the credential as an indivisible object reproduces the all-or-nothing plastic card and throws away the cryptographic advantage.

Use case. Bar entry reads only 'Age over 18 (required)'. Non-requested attributes do not appear at all.

T6 Consent & Disclosure Decision
BP-06 Predicate proofs rendered as human statements
10/10

Show the proposition being proven and its truth value, not a field with a value. Best form: 'Over 18: Yes. Your date of birth is not shared.'

Why. Only that form teaches the privacy property while exercising it; the negative clause does as much work as the positive.

Use case. Age-gated retail, EUDI age attestation, online age assurance under UK OSA / EU DSA.

T5 Request Comprehension
BP-07 Clear signing: render effect, never the digest
10/10

Blind signing is the canonical trust-UX failure. EIP-712 (typed data), ERC-7730 (display metadata), ERC-8213 (what the wallet must display) form the remediation stack. Never present a hash as a description; a 'show raw' affordance for experts, never the default.

Why. Never ask a person to sign what they cannot read.

Use case. A Permit2 approval renders as 'You are allowing [contract] to spend up to 50 USDC until 14 August 2026.' Unlimited allowance gets a distinct, louder treatment.

T5 Request Comprehension
BP-08 Outcome simulation and before/after preview
10/10

Simulate the transaction and show the projected balance and permission deltas before commit. It reframes the screen from 'do you authorize this instruction' to 'here is what will be true afterward.'

Why. Shifts the cognitive task from parsing to comparing, which humans do vastly better. The largest single unexploited pattern transfer between crypto and identity wallets.

Use case. Identity equivalent: 'After this, [org] will know your surname, your city, and that you hold a valid Swiss residence permit. They will not learn your date of birth or address.'

T5 Request Comprehension
BP-09 Fixed-level progressive disclosure (L1 to L4)
10/10

C2PA defines four named disclosure levels: L1 Indicator (persistent, scannable mark), L2 Summary (compact card), L3 Detail (full provenance, navigable), L4 Forensic (complete technical detail).

Why. The naming gives a cross-vendor vocabulary. Most progressive disclosure guidance is a principle; this is a schema, and schemas let ecosystems converge.

Use case. Adopt L1-L4 as the disclosure-depth axis for any trust interface, including AI trust scoring.

T5 Request Comprehension
BP-10 Separate computed facts from asserted claims
10/10

Distinguish assertions created by the signer under system control (trusted timestamps, device-signed GPS) from assertions gathered from elsewhere (imported EXIF, typed captions). Label them distinctly; never present as equivalent.

Why. The single most transferable idea in the corpus: any interface mixing measured, derived and declared inputs into one treatment is lying by typography. Applies directly to AI trust scoring.

Use case. 'Recorded by the capture device' versus 'Entered by the creator.'

T5 Request Comprehension

Layer C · Calibrating the human

BP-11 Friction proportional to irreversibility
9/10

Assign each action an irreversibility cost and spend interaction friction against it: reversible low-stakes get none; irreversible get typed confirmation, biometric re-auth, or a delay.

Why. Friction spent on reversible actions is friction stolen from irreversible ones. Confirmation dialogs are a finite budget, not a free safety measure.

Use case. Irreversible plus a live threat signal: block, with a deliberately hard override.

T6 Consent & Disclosure DecisionT7 Execution & Confirmation
BP-12 Warnings as a scarce resource with a budget
9/10

High-frequency, low-specificity alerts produce habituation and lower trust in the warning system itself. Use simple messages with evidence, deliberate blocking vs non-blocking, polymorphic warnings, required input for the top tier.

Why. Alert fatigue is real and measured.

Use case. Set a target warning rate per active user per month and treat exceeding it as a defect, like a crash rate.

T9 Incident, Recovery & Revocation
BP-13 Named uncertainty, with the stakes attached
9/10

Never present a probabilistic output with the same weight as a fact, nor the same confidence label across different stakes. Three required components: the confidence signal, what drives it, and a different UI state/action at each threshold.

Why. 75% confidence means something entirely different for a product recommendation than a compliance flag. Below a usable threshold the correct output is a human-review prompt, not a low number.

Use case. Directly relevant to an AI trust-score surface.

T5 Request Comprehension
BP-14 Progressive delegation: autonomy earned
9/10

Start an autonomous system with narrow authority and expand it as the user's approval history demonstrates reliability: approve every action, then batch-approve routine categories, then notify-only, with demotion available at any time.

Why. The trust-calibration curve made operational; the delegation history is itself the audit trail. Final-step human approval alone is a documented, exploited bypass, so expose the bounds (limits, allowlists, scopes) as first-class.

Use case. Agentic commerce and delegated wallet authority; mandate-based flows with signed instructions and daily spend limits.

T6 Consent & Disclosure Decision
BP-15 Skeuomorphic anchoring for novel credentials
8/10

swiyu renders the credential with a monospace typeface evoking the Swiss passport and holographic accent colours, while the wallet shell uses sans-serif. The credential looks like a document; the container looks like an app.

Why. Imports thirty years of institutional trust in the physical artifact, and separates the thing-being-trusted from the thing-doing-the-holding.

Use case. Caution: security-document visual language on a self-asserted credential would be trust theatre of the worst kind.

T1 Invitation & Onboarding

Layer D · Making trust reversible

BP-16 The disclosure receipt
10/10

Every disclosure event produces a durable, human-readable, exportable record: timestamp, counterparty identity, exact attributes released, legal basis, retention claimed, transcript reference. Presented as a searchable activity log.

Why. The highest-leverage under-built pattern: the only thing that makes a subject-access request tractable, the only evidence in a dispute, and the only way a person can learn their own disclosure behaviour.

Use case. Almost no shipping wallet does this well.

T8 Retrospection & Portfolio
BP-17 Symmetric effort for grant and withdrawal
10/10

If granting takes one tap, withdrawing takes one tap. Asymmetric consent is now legal exposure under EDPB dark-pattern categories, DSA Art. 25, and FTC Section 5.

Why. The 2025 CPPA action against American Honda (extensive form to opt out) is the reference case.

Use case. Design test: count the taps, screens, and fields in each direction. If unequal, the design is obstructing.

T8 Retrospection & Portfolio
BP-18 Recovery designed before onboarding
10/10

Present the recovery model before key generation. State the three non-obvious rules at generation (order matters; nobody legitimate asks for it; there is no reset). Offer social/threshold recovery as a first-class named quorum. Rehearse a recovery drill in onboarding.

Why. The most consistent finding in the custody literature: users misunderstand what the seed phrase is and what happens on device loss (CHI 2025, n=643; Ontario n=47).

Use case. '3 of 5 trusted contacts', not an advanced setting.

T3 Custody & Recovery Setup
BP-19 Every warning carries a recourse path
9/10

Every risk state needs at least three exits: proceed with informed acceptance, abandon safely, and dispute. MetaMask's false-positive reporting flow is the reference implementation.

Why. A warning that dead-ends teaches users that warnings are noise. The dispute path makes the trust system accountable, not merely authoritative.

Use case. A form of the redress that regulation (AI Act) increasingly presumes exists.

T9 Incident, Recovery & Revocation
BP-20 State what the system does not prove
9/10

Trust systems accumulate over-attribution. C2PA does not detect deepfakes, does not detect AI generation absent disclosure, and does not establish truth. Provenance is not veracity.

Why. The interface is where over-attribution forms, so the interface is where it must be corrected, at L2, in one plain sentence, not in the FAQ.

Use case. A verified issuer is not a good actor; a trust score is not a guarantee.

Mainstream products that solve each moment well, one or more per moment, each linking to the official page.

1Invitation & OnboardingHold

3Relationship Formation & RecognitionHold
  • Safety numbers ↗ Signal

    QR or numeric comparison over a separate channel with a local verified state, stating plainly that it authenticates the encrypted relationship, not real-world identity.

  • Cross-signing / emoji verification ↗ Matrix

6Counterparty Identification & VerificationHold

  • OAuth consent screen ↗ Googlealso Request Comprehension

  • App installation & authorization ↗ GitHub

    Visually distinguishes requested repository reach from account permissions and advises the user to verify the app's owner.

7Request ComprehensionHold

The prescriptive rules

Rules from named authorities, law down to expert guidance

Prescriptive rules from named authorities, each with a citable verbatim quote and an authority level from law down to expert guidance, grouped by moment.

Invitation & Onboarding9 rules
Regulator guidance avoidP-33
Fickle: do not vary your data-protection interface between contexts.
“the design of the interface is inconsistent and not clear, making it hard for users to navigate the different data protection control tools”
Category 5 - Fickle (sub-patterns: Lacking hierarchy, Decontextualising, Inconsistent interface, Language discontinuity) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance avoidP-34
Left in the dark: do not hide controls or leave the user unsure how data is processed.
“an interface is designed in a way to hide information or data protection control tools or to leave users unsure of how their data is processed”
Category 6 - Left in the dark (sub-patterns: Conflicting information, Ambiguous wording or information) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance P-35
Use one term for one concept across the entire ecosystem, and define any unavoidable jargon in place.
“Across the website, the same wording and definition is used for the same data protection”
Best practice - Coherent wordings · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance P-36
Deliver short contextual explanations at the decision point, in addition to - not instead of - the full policy.
“in addition to an exhaustive privacy policy, bring short bits of information at the most appropriate time for the user”
Best practice - Contextual information · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board

Why. Progressive disclosure applied to legal text.

Regulator guidance P-37
When you change a privacy notice, keep prior versions accessible and highlight what changed.
“When changes are made to the privacy notice, make previous versions accessible with date of release and highlight changes”
Best practice - Change spotting and comparison · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Expert guidance P-38
When offering a choice of identity providers or wallets, make every option visually equal in size and weight.
“Do design sign-in options that are uniform in width and height”
Sign-in / sign-out · Login.gov developer user-experience guidance · General Services Administration / 18F, USA

Why. Unequal styling in a provider chooser is a thumb on the scale. Uniformity is auditable.

Expert guidance P-39
Do not use an external-link icon on a sign-in control - it reads as leaving for an untrusted site.
“Don't use the external-link icon that might suggest a link to another website”
Sign-in / sign-out · Login.gov developer user-experience guidance · General Services Administration / 18F, USA

Why. A genuine trust-signal rule with a stated reason: the icon imports the wrong mental model at the moment of authentication.

Expert guidance P-40
Never use an icon alone where a text label carries the meaning.
“Don't use an icon instead of a text label”
Sign-in / sign-out · Login.gov developer user-experience guidance · General Services Administration / 18F, USA
Regulator guidance P-49
Treat transparency as making available WHEN AI is used, HOW it works, and WHAT it can and cannot do, tailored to who is asking.
“In its broadest meaning, transparency refers to the availability of information about AI systems, such as when they are being used, how they work, and their capabilities and limitations.”
What is transparency, why does it matter? · Enhancing Trust in AI Through Increased Transparency · Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2026)

Why. Different audiences need different transparency: a consumer needs to know they are dealing with AI; a regulator needs design and performance data. One disclosure does not serve all.

Counterparty Identification & Verification5 rules
Law P-01
Name the relying party and list exactly what will be shared BEFORE the user authorises, every time.
“When a user initiates presentation of 1 or more credentials (fully or partially), the facilitation mechanism must notify the user of all the following: (1) the personal or organisational information to be presented; and (2) the relying party to whom the personal or organisational information is being presented, where not being presented in-person.”
Rule 11(4) · Digital Identity Services Trust Framework Rules 2024 (consolidated) · Department of Internal Affairs / Public Service Commission, New Zealand

Why. Users decide about the counterparty before they decide about the data. A disclosure UI that leads with attributes answers the second question first.

Industry mandate P-02
Display the relying party's trading or brand name - the name the user would recognise - not its legal entity name or client ID.
“AISPs must display the company's trading name/brand name (i.e. the Client Name) to the PSU during the setup and revocation of consent.”
AIS Consent · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Legal entity names are frequently unrecognisable to the people who must judge them. Recognition is the whole function of the disclosure.

Industry mandate P-03
Give the user a way to tell your genuine authentication screen from a spoof, such as a personalised element only you could know.
“Identifying your firm as genuine - For example have personalised greetings during authentication so that the PSU knows they are authenticating with their own ASPSP and not a fake.”
CEG Checklist Ref 3 · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Redirection flows train users to expect a hand-off to an unfamiliar screen, which is exactly the condition phishing exploits.

Law P-04
Warn the user to share only with relying parties they recognise, at the moment of sharing.
“a warning about sharing their information only to relying parties they know”
Rule 11 (facilitation mechanisms) · Digital Identity Services Trust Framework Rules 2024 (consolidated) · Department of Internal Affairs / Public Service Commission, New Zealand

Why. A wallet cannot verify that a legitimate-but-unknown verifier is appropriate for this user; only the user can.

Regulator guidance P-50
Disclose when a person is interacting with an AI system rather than a human, wherever they could reasonably be unaware.
“An individual using a chatbot to generate audiovisual content would almost certainly know that they are interacting with an AI system, whereas a consumer engaging with a customer service chatbot that is not identified as such may be unaware that they are interacting with a machine, rather than a human.”
AI Interaction · Enhancing Trust in AI Through Increased Transparency · Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2026)

Why. Whether disclosure matters is contextual: redundant when the AI is obvious, load-bearing when the system could be mistaken for a person. Over-disclosing everywhere causes its own fatigue.

Request Comprehension2 rules
Regulator guidance P-51
Publish a system's capabilities, limitations and appropriate uses (model / system cards) so users can judge fitness and organisations can procure responsibly.
“Model cards typically describe a model's capabilities, limitations, and appropriate uses, helping prospective users determine whether it is suited to a task, while system cards describe how a deployed system behaves in practice, including the safeguards built around it.”
Information about AI Systems · Enhancing Trust in AI Through Increased Transparency · Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2026)

Why. Deployed outside its intended context and without stated limits, a capable model is routinely trusted where it should not be. The interface must carry what the system does NOT do.

Regulator guidance P-52
For AI-generated content, mark origin durably and carry provenance in metadata, not on a visible label alone.
“Many generative AI developers now mark AI-generated content with hidden watermarks and include provenance information in metadata.”
AI-Generated Content · Enhancing Trust in AI Through Increased Transparency · Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2026)

Why. Watermarks are brittle and easy to strip, so durable provenance (C2PA-style) must back the visible cue. Labelling frequent, expected AI edits also produces banner blindness.

Consent & Disclosure Decision16 rules
Industry mandate P-05
Group requested data into named clusters at the top level and let the user drill down to individual fields. Do not present a flat list of raw attribute names.
“Both AISPs and ASPSPs must describe the data being shared at a Data Cluster level and allow the PSU to 'drill-down' to see the detail at Permission level”
Permissions & Data Clusters · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Progressive disclosure applied to the attribute list itself. A flat list of 14 technical field names is unreadable; four named groups are.

Industry mandate P-06
Request only the data the service actually needs, and suppress request categories that are irrelevant to the product.
“The AISP must ensure they have business rules that manage the relationship between data cluster to product type and omit access to data clusters that are irrelevant”
Permissions & Data Clusters · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Data minimisation enforced at the request-construction layer rather than left to the user's refusal.

Industry mandate P-07
State explicitly whether any third party beyond the immediate recipient will get the data.
“AISPs must provide PSUs with sufficient information to enable PSUs to make an informed decision, for example, detail the purpose for which the data will be used (including whether any other parties will have access to the information) the period over which it has been requested and when the consent for the account information will expire”
AIS Consent · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Onward sharing is the disclosure users most consistently fail to anticipate, and it is routinely omitted from consent screens.

Law P-08
Never bundle unrelated permissions into the transaction the user came to complete.
“Trust Framework providers must not require the user to provide authorisation, consent or permission for any activity not directly related to completing the accredited digital identity service being undertaken.”
Rule 10 · Digital Identity Services Trust Framework Rules 2024 (consolidated) · Department of Internal Affairs / Public Service Commission, New Zealand

Why. Bundling converts a specific consent into a general one by exploiting the user's need to finish the task.

Statutory code P-09
Default every optional data setting to the most protective value. If you deviate, you must be able to justify it.
“Settings must be 'high privacy' by default (unless you can demonstrate a compelling reason for a different default setting, taking account of the best interests of the child).”
Standard 7 - Default settings · Age appropriate design: a code of practice for online services (Children's Code) · Information Commissioner's Office, UK

Why. Defaults are the single most powerful determinant of outcome because most users never change them.

Statutory code P-10
Do not use nudges, visual weighting or emotive language to steer users toward disclosing more.
“Do not use nudge techniques to lead or encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data or weaken or turn off their privacy protections.”
Standard 13 - Nudge techniques · Age appropriate design: a code of practice for online services (Children's Code) · Information Commissioner's Office, UK

Why. Steering is measurable and auditable in a way that 'clarity' is not.

Industry mandate P-11
Ask for consent once, at the party that has the context to explain it. Do not re-ask at the credential holder.
“While an ASPSP may provide additional information and clarification throughout the journey, at no stage should the ASPSP seek to reconfirm or check that the PSU wants the TPP to perform the activity they have consented to.”
CEG Checklist Ref 2 · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Treats re-confirmation as a DEFECT rather than a safety feature. The party that can explain the consent captures it; the credential holder authenticates identity only.

Industry mandate P-12
Give continuing and declining equal visual prominence and equal path length.
“AISPs must allow the PSU to explicitly reconfirm their consent with equal prominence given to continue or discontinue the service”
90-day reconfirmation · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Symmetry is a measurable, auditable design commitment. 'Clarity' is not.

Expert guidance P-13
Make withdrawing consent exactly as easy as giving it.
“the process for withdrawing consent is as easy for the Subject as providing consent.”
CONS 8 · Pan-Canadian Trust Framework - Notice & Consent, and Digital Wallet Component Overview · DIACC, Canada

Why. Asymmetry between entry and exit is the defining property of a roach-motel dark pattern.

Industry mandate P-14
Cap the exit path at one friction screen, then confirm.
“Once the PSU has clicked 'Disconnect', the AISP should only provide one screen prior to cancelling... A confirmation screen should be provided after this”
AIS Consent Dashboard · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Converts 'don't obstruct' into a countable limit. One screen is legitimate confirmation; two or more is obstruction.

Industry mandate P-15
On revocation, state plainly what happens to data already shared.
“The confirmation screen must confirm what happens to any existing data... AISPs must ensure that they are fair and transparent about how existing data is processed”
AIS Consent Dashboard · UK Open Banking Customer Experience Guidelines (CEG) and CEG Checklist · Open Banking Limited

Why. Users routinely assume revocation is retroactive deletion. It usually is not, and the gap between assumption and reality is where trust breaks.

Regulator guidance avoidP-29
Overloading: do not bury the protective choice under an avalanche of options.
“users are confronted with an avalanche/large quantity of requests, information, options or possibilities”
Category 1 - Overloading (sub-patterns: Continuous prompting, Privacy Maze, Too many options) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board

Why. Volume itself is the manipulation. Every option is individually defensible; the aggregate is not.

Regulator guidance avoidP-30
Skipping: do not design the journey so the data-protection decision is passed over.
“designing the interface or user journey in a way that the users forget or do not think about all or some of the data protection aspects”
Category 2 - Skipping (sub-patterns: Deceptive snugness, Look over there) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance avoidP-31
Stirring: do not use emotional framing or visual nudges to move the disclosure decision.
“affects the choice users would make by appealing to their emotions or using visual nudges”
Category 3 - Stirring (sub-patterns: Emotional Steering, Hidden in plain sight) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance avoidP-32
Obstructing: do not make the protective action hard or impossible to complete.
“an obstruction or blocking of users in their process of getting informed or managing their data by making the action hard or impossible to achieve”
Category 4 - Obstructing (sub-patterns: Dead end, Longer than necessary, Misleading action) · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board
Regulator guidance P-47
Treat every prompt as spending a finite attention budget, and be able to state what each one buys.
“users are confronted with an avalanche/large quantity of requests”
Category 1 - Overloading, sub-pattern 4.1.1 Continuous prompting · EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces · European Data Protection Board

Why. The regulator and the economics agree: repeated prompting is both a compliance failure and an attention-budget failure.

Regulator guidance P-53
Record AI incidents and report serious ones, so causes and trends are understood in time to mitigate proactively rather than reactively.
“As AI is increasingly integrated into products, services, and processes, a better and more timely understanding of AI incidents along with their causes and trends becomes increasingly important.”
AI Incidents · Enhancing Trust in AI Through Increased Transparency · Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (2026)

Why. Incident visibility today leans on voluntary public reports, leaving governments, researchers and the public with an incomplete picture of how often AI failures occur and how well they are handled.

Regulator guidance P-44
Always offer more than one route to prove the same thing.
“You should always make sure users have more than one way to use your product or service.”
Trust framework - inclusion · UK digital identity and attributes trust framework · Department for Science, Innovation and Technology / OfDIA, UK

Why. Single-route verification silently excludes whoever lacks that route.

Statutory code P-45
Tell the user, unmistakably, whenever they are being monitored or tracked.
“If your online service allows a parent or carer to monitor their child's online activity or track their location, provide an obvious sign to the child when they are being monitored.”
Standard 11 - Parental controls · Age appropriate design: a code of practice for online services (Children's Code) · Information Commissioner's Office, UK

Why. The archetypal trust-signal rule: covert observation is the failure, and the signal must be present at the time, not disclosed in a policy.

Expert guidance P-46
For age thresholds using estimation, apply an explicit safety buffer and publish demographic-split accuracy.
“It is important for facial age estimation algorithms to be independently evaluated”

Why. A buffer (e.g. requiring an estimated 25+ to pass an 18+ check) trades false-accept risk against false-reject exclusion, and that trade should be stated numerically rather than hidden.

Key findings

C2PA forbids the traffic-light pattern

All disclosure label categories render in one uniform colour to signal neutrality, not a scale and explicitly not green/red. A scale would imply a value judgement about whether AI involvement was good or bad. Use colour scales for risk (scalar); uniform colour for provenance categories (categorical).

Disclosure has a budget

C2PA examined an 'AI-enhanced' label and rejected it: the enhancement is frequent and expected, so the label would surface constantly, become redundant, and lose meaning. Every warning spends from a finite attention budget.

The waterline is where trust UX fails

Governance facts (is this verifier registered, for what purpose, under whose authority) are the hardest to render and the most consequential to omit. Products render the Agency and Engineering tips well and leave the straddling Governance layer invisible.

The market's weakest moments are T4, T5, T8

Counterparty identification, request comprehension, and retrospection are the least-solved across shipping products. T3 (custody) is best-studied and still unsolved; T9 (repair) is almost entirely unaddressed outside a few crypto wallets.

The biggest unexploited transfer: simulation to identity

The crypto-wallet 'here is what will be true afterward' reframing is the strongest calibration device in the corpus and has never been evaluated for identity attribute disclosure. The most promising single experiment.

Design for calibrated reliance, not maximal trust

A trust moment is a decision under uncertainty, and the goal is confidence that matches real reliability. Over-trust (misuse) and under-trust (disuse) are both failures. Every moment carries a default posture, Increase / Hold / Decrease / Suspend, and every increase in authority, disclosure or irreversibility must be matched by an increase in evidence, clarity or control.

AI-content labels are brittle and can over-fire

Canada's transparency consultation notes text watermarks remain easy to remove, so durable provenance (C2PA-style metadata) has to back any visible cue. Labelling frequent, expected AI edits produces banner blindness, the same finite-attention problem the disclosure-budget finding describes.

The anti-pattern register (trust theatre)

AP-01

The unattributed badge

A 'Verified' mark with no path to who verified, against what criteria, when

Seen in: Marketplaces, profile systems, AI vendor listings

AP-02

Binary trust

Two states only, so absence of warning reads as safety

Seen in: Most identity wallets today

AP-03

Consent theatre

A single unbundled all-or-nothing choice, styled as control

Seen in: mDL and eID pilots; app permission flows

AP-04

Hash as description

Presenting a digest, address, or DID as if it conveyed meaning

Seen in: Hardware wallets, early SSI wallets

AP-05

Warning inflation

Alerting on everything, so users learn to dismiss reflexively

Seen in: Enterprise security tooling; browser interstitials

AP-06

Confidence laundering

Rendering a low-confidence output with high-confidence styling

Seen in: Nearly all consumer AI features

AP-07

Asymmetric revocation

One tap to grant, six screens to withdraw

Seen in: Consent management platforms

AP-08

Skeuomorphic overreach

Institutional security language on a self-asserted claim

Seen in: Web3 'credentials', badge systems

AP-09

Amnesia by design

No record of what was shared with whom

Seen in: Most wallets, most SSO

AP-10

The dead-end warning

Risk flagged, no path to dispute, abandon, or understand

Seen in: Fraud and risk scoring UIs

AP-11

Sovereignty without affordances

Control claimed in marketing, not matched by disclosure, revocation, or portability

Seen in: Much of the SSI sector

AP-12

Novelty opacity

Failing to explain what the new thing is; users default to old mental models

Seen in: Documented in Sartor et al. (ECIS 2022)

Open questions

Gaps where original work would be additive
  1. Does the disclosure receipt change behaviour?
    No published study tests whether a durable, reviewable log of one's own disclosures changes subsequent disclosure decisions. Testable, cheap, a strong contribution.
  2. Do purpose-mismatch warnings work?
    eIDAS 2.0 makes registered scope machine-checkable. Nobody has tested whether surfacing a scope mismatch at the consent moment changes behaviour, or just produces another habituated warning.
  3. Does transaction simulation transfer to identity disclosure?
    The strongest calibration device in the corpus, never evaluated for identity attribute disclosure. The most promising single experiment identified.
  4. The implied truth effect in provenance labelling
    C2PA flags it in the spec as an open user-research question. Unresolved and directly relevant to AI content-disclosure regimes now in force.
  5. Progressive-delegation curves for agentic authority
    How fast should autonomy expand, on what evidence, and what must the demotion affordance look like? Almost no empirical work exists; the deployment wave is arriving now.
